CAUSES: Threats to Our Moana

Understanding the human impacts that disrupt our ocean ecosystems is the first step toward becoming active kaitiaki.

Plastic Pollution & Ocean Waste

1. Plastic Pollution & Marine Debris

Every year, over 8 million tons of plastic waste make their way into our oceans.

From single-use packaging to abandoned fishing gear ("ghost nets"), plastic debris broken down into microplastics enters the marine food chain, harming sealife, birds, and ecosystems.

Plastic is a permanent human pollutant that disrupts the natural balance (mauri) of ecosystems.

Practicing stewardship means reducing our reliance on single-use waste and removing existing litter before it reaches the sea.


Overfishing & Unsustainable Methods

2. Overfishing & Destructive Fishing

Commercial overfishing drains marine populations faster than they can naturally recover, leaving species unable to replenish.

Furthermore, destructive practices like bottom trawling destroy delicate coral reefs and seafloor habitats, capturing unintended sea life as bycatch.

Traditional kaitiakitanga involves sustainable harvesting practices, such as using seasonal rāhui (temporary bans or restrictions) to allow marine environments and fish stocks time to regenerate.


Climate Change & Ocean Acidification

3. Climate Change & Rising Sea Temperatures

Our oceans absorb over 90% of the excess heat created by greenhouse gas emissions.

This leads to ocean warming, coral bleaching, and severe weather events.

Additionally, as oceans absorb excess carbon dioxide, seawater becomes more acidic, making it difficult for shell-building organisms and plankton to survive.

Protecting our climate is essential to protecting the ocean.

Recognising our interconnection (whanaungatanga) with nature reminds us that atmospheric changes directly threaten all marine species.


Coastal Pollution & Runoff

4. Chemical Runoff & Sedimentation

Harmful chemicals, fertilizers, heavy metals, and soil erosion run off from farmland and cities directly into rivers and coastal waters.

This causes toxic algal blooms, creates oxygen-depleted "dead zones," and smothers fragile coastal ecosystems.

In a traditional worldview, water flows from the mountains to the sea (mai i ngā maunga ki te moana).

Protecting the ocean requires taking care of land and waterways upstream to maintain holistic environmental health.
